# Trends since the restart (Oct 3 – Oct 20, 2026)

Data: every Short on the channel (116) with public views read Oct 21, 00:10 UTC (`Posted Tracker (Oct 21, 2026).csv`), likes where YouTube served them (half the per-video reads were bot-blocked), the Oct 17 Studio export ("stayed to watch", 22 new-era Shorts), and `Tools/data/channel_log.csv` for subscribers.
Eras: **old** = 72 NBA Shorts, Nov 14 2025 – Feb 23 2026; **new** = 36 Shorts since the restart on Oct 3 2026 (35 aged, plus #48 posted tonight); 8 early AI-comedy Shorts are left out.
Evidence tiers: [V] measured here, [S] supported but small sample, [H] hypothesis.

## 1. The big change: fewer views per Short, but better viewers
| | Old era (72) | New era (35, excl. #48) |
|---|---|---|
| Median views | **30,000** | **3,600** |
| Shorts at 20K+ | 59 of 72 | 4 of 35 |
| Median like rate | 0.87% | **1.58%** |
| Subscribers gained | 469 in total (whole history to Sep 26) | **+82 in 17 days** (469 → 551) |
| Subs per 1K views | about 0.2 | **about 0.36** |

- [V] Typical views are about 8x lower than the old era. The old era was strikingly uniform (monthly medians 18.5K, 32.5K, 31.5K, 28.5K): the feed gave almost every Short a ~30K run.
- [V] The new Shorts convert better: almost twice the like rate and nearly twice the subscribers per view. People who see them like them more.
- [H] Why the views dropped is not provable from public data: the old Shorts had 8-11 months to collect views, the channel was silent for 7 months (Feb 23 – Oct 3), and the format changed (old: a clip plus a caption; new: a researched story with a voice). The Studio export of an old-era Short at the same age would settle it.

## 2. One number separates breakouts from the rest: "stayed to watch"
- [V] Across the 22 new-era Shorts in the Studio export, **stayed % and views move together (Spearman 0.83)**.
- [V] The 5 Shorts at **73%+ stayed** have a median of **21,000 views**; the 17 below 73% have **2,800**. #42 had the channel record (84.3%) and reached 25K.
- [S] So the first second (frame 1 + the first spoken line) decides reach. Everything below is a way to raise that number.

## 4. Stars
- [V] LeBron/Bronny are in 11 of 35 new Shorts (31%): median 3.9K vs 3.2K for the rest, so there's no penalty on average, but only #42 broke out, and the Bronny-only Shorts are the weakest (5.7K, 3.7K, 1.9K, 197).
- [S] Wemby (27K, 9.8K) and Flagg (21K, 10K) are the strongest names per Short so far (n=2 each).

## 5. Timing and cadence
- [V] Weekly medians: Oct 3-6 4.4K, Oct 7-10 2.9K, Oct 11-14 5.6K, Oct 15-17 6.7K, Oct 18-20 3.8K (young). **No fatigue trend.** The "last 7 down" reading on Oct 20 was #42 and #40 leaving the 7-Short window.
- [V] Three of the four breakouts came in the first four days (Oct 3-6), then none for 10 days, then #42. The middle of the pack sits at 1.5-6K: the feed's first test. About 1 in 9 new Shorts escapes it.
- [V] Posting 1, 2 or 3 a day made no visible difference (days with one post: median 3.8K; all days: 3.6K). Small sample.

## 6. What to do with this
1. **Keep the bar at "would a non-fan stop for this?"**: one famous name, one number that sounds wrong, real footage of that exact thing in second 1. That is what the 73%+ Shorts share.
2. **Lead with THE #42 FORMAT and size/record oddities**; use news (like tonight's opening-night games) when it has a record or a number gap, not just a result.
3. **Rotate stars**: fewer Bronny-only stories; Wemby, Flagg, Clark, SGA and Jokic are underused.
4. **No re-uploads.** If a Short flops, make a new angle, don't post the same one twice.
5. **Measure stayed % per Short**: the one-time YouTube sign-in (Tools Guide, "Daily retention, automatically") or a weekly Studio export turns this from 22 data points into all of them.
